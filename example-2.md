Adverse Possession: Vendor's Position — Legal Analysis
Jurisdiction: England and Wales throughout.

1. Adverse Possession under the Limitation Act 1980 and the Pre-LRA 2002 Regime
The substantive claim
Under s 15(1) of the Limitation Act 1980 (LA 1980):

"No action shall be brought by any person to recover any land after the expiration of twelve years from the date on which the right of action accrued to him..."

Adverse possession began around 1986. The twelve-year period therefore expired around 1998, some five years before the Land Registration Act 2002 (LRA 2002) came into force.

To establish adverse possession the vendor must satisfy two elements confirmed by the House of Lords in JA Pye (Oxford) Ltd v Graham [2002] UKHL 30:

Factual possession — sole, physical possession of the land, treating it as one's own to the exclusion of all others (including the paper owner): Powell v McFarlane (1977) 38 P&CR 452, per Slade J at 471
Animus possidendi — an intention to possess the land as one's own, not by permission: JA Pye, per Lord Browne-Wilkinson at [40]–[41]
Forty years' exclusive use enclosed within a physical fence provides strong evidence of both. The vendor's solicitor should nonetheless obtain a statutory declaration dealing explicitly with each element.

Critical threshold question: was the strip registered or unregistered?
The legal consequence of the 12-year period expiring differs depending on whether the council's title was registered.

A. If the council's title was registered (most likely for local authority land in England)

Under the Land Registration Act 1925 (LRA 1925) s 75(1), set out by Lewison LJ in Rashid v Nasrullah [2018] EWCA Civ 2685 at [9]:

"The Limitation Acts shall apply to registered land in the same manner and to the same extent as those Acts apply to land not registered, except that where, if the land were not registered, the estate of the person registered as proprietor would be extinguished, such estate shall not be extinguished but shall be deemed to be held by the proprietor for the time being in trust for the person who, by virtue of the said Acts, has acquired title against any proprietor..."

When the 12-year period expired around 1998, the council did not forfeit its registered title; instead it became bare trustee of the strip for the adverse possessor. An equitable interest in the strip vested in the adverse possessor at that point.

B. If the council's title was unregistered

Under LA 1980 s 17:

"at the expiration of the period prescribed by this Act for any person to bring an action to recover land...the title of that person to the land shall be extinguished."

If unregistered, the council's title was extinguished outright around 1998 and the adverse possessor's possessory freehold arose automatically. This produces an even stronger outcome for the vendor: there is simply no title left to contest.

Note on limitation period for local authorities: Under LA 1980 sch 1 para 10, the Crown benefits from a 30-year period, not 12. Local authorities are not the Crown for this purpose; the standard 12-year period under s 15(1) applies to them.

Note on acknowledgment: Under LA 1980 s 29(1)–(2), if the adverse possessor ever acknowledged the council's title in writing signed by them or their agent, the period restarts from that date. Any conveyance correspondence, rent payment, or letter accepting the council's ownership would be fatal to the claim. This must be verified.

2. LRA 2002 Transitional Provisions — Rights Crystallised Before 13 October 2003
Commencement
The LRA 2002 came into force on 13 October 2003 pursuant to the Land Registration Act 2002 (Commencement No. 4) Order 2003 (SI 2003/1725).

The new regime going forward
Under LRA 2002 s 96(1):

"No period of limitation under section 15 of the Limitation Act 1980...shall run against any person, other than a chargee, in relation to an estate in land...the title to which is registered."

By s 96(3), LA 1980 s 17 accordingly does not extinguish a registered proprietor's title. The entirely new application-and-notification scheme is contained in sch 6 (introduced by s 97).

The transitional saving — Schedule 12, paragraph 18
The critical provision for this case is LRA 2002 sch 12 para 18. Its text, extracted from Rashid v Nasrullah [2018] EWCA Civ 2685 at [10] (Lewison LJ), reads:

"(1) Where a registered estate in land is held in trust for a person by virtue of section 75(1) of the Land Registration Act 1925 immediately before the coming into force of section 97, he is entitled to be registered as the proprietor of the estate.
(2) A person has a defence to any action for the possession of land (in addition to any other defence he may have) if he is entitled under this paragraph to be registered as the proprietor of an estate in the land.
(3) Where in an action for possession of land a court determines that a person is entitled to a defence under this paragraph, the court must order the registrar to register him as the proprietor of the estate in relation to which he is entitled under this paragraph to be registered."

Applying this to the facts:

Point	Analysis
Was the estate held on trust under LRA 1925 s 75(1)?	Yes — the trust arose around 1998 when the 12-year period expired
Was it held on trust immediately before s 97 came into force (13 October 2003)?	Yes — there is no evidence of any intervening act by the council to recover possession or obtain a court order before that date
Consequence	The adverse possessor is entitled to be registered as proprietor under sch 12 para 18(1); has a statutory defence to any possession action under para 18(2); and a court must order registration if the defence succeeds under para 18(3)
As Lewison LJ confirmed in Rashid at [2]:

"Under that regime [the LRA 1925] it was possible to acquire title to registered land by adverse possession, if the registered proprietor had been dispossessed for 12 years. In that event he was deemed to hold his registered title on trust for the squatter. It is necessary to emphasise the fact that since the coming into force of the Land Registration Act 2002 the legal landscape as regards adverse possession has been radically changed. This case is concerned with events that all took place before that change."

The practical significance of sch 12 para 18: The vendor does not need to go through the new ten-year Schedule 6 adverse possession regime (which requires application, notification of the registered proprietor, and the risk of counter-notice). The entitlement to registration crystallised in 1998 and was preserved on 13 October 2003. It exists now, and the council's paper title on the register is held on trust for the adverse possessor. The council's right to recover the strip was extinguished before the new regime ever applied.

3. The Council's Right to Inspect
There is no general statutory right for a local authority to enter and inspect land it claims to own but does not possess. Registration as proprietor does not itself confer a right of physical entry against a person in established adverse possession, particularly one with an entitlement to registration under sch 12 para 18.

Entry onto land in another's possession without authority is a trespass at common law.

The following are the principal powers a council might attempt to invoke and their limits:

Statute	Provision	Scope	Applicable?
Local Government Act 1972	s 222	Power to bring or defend legal proceedings — not a right of entry	No
Local Government Act 1972	s 111	Incidental power to do anything conducive to discharge of functions — cannot create rights of entry that do not otherwise exist	No
Localism Act 2011	s 1	General power of competence — gives flexibility in how functions are exercised; cannot override established property rights	No
Town and Country Planning Act 1990	s 196A	Right of entry for planning survey purposes, on 24 hours' notice	Only if for a genuine planning purpose; not ownership investigation
Housing Act 2004	Pt 1	Entry for housing conditions inspection	Inapplicable to an open strip of land
Highways Act 1980	ss 289–291	Highway inspection powers	Only if the strip is alleged to be adopted highway
What the vendor's solicitor should do: Write formally to the council's legal department requiring it to identify:

the exact statutory provision conferring a right of entry;
the stated purpose of the proposed inspection; and
the notice period proposed.
If the council cannot cite valid statutory authority, entry should be refused. The vendor should not grant "courteous access": doing so without express reservation could be treated by the council as an implicit acknowledgment of its ownership claim. Any letter refusing access should note, without prejudice to the main adverse possession position, that the vendor is in the process of applying to HMLR to rectify the register under sch 12 para 18.

If the council threatens to seek a court order for access, it would need to demonstrate a subsisting legal or equitable interest capable of grounding such relief — a near-impossible task given the adverse possession timeline.

4. Practical Steps Before Exchange
Step 1 — Identify the title status of the strip (immediate)

Commission a search of the index map (HMLR form SIM) to determine whether the strip:
falls within a separately registered title in the council's name (if so, obtain the title number);
falls within the vendor's own registered title plan (if so, the council's claim may be unsupported on the register); or
is unregistered (opening the LA 1980 s 17 route)
Compare the title plan against the physical fenced boundary on the ground
Step 2 — Obtain statutory declarations

Vendor to swear a statutory declaration covering: commencement of possession (~1986), nature and exclusivity of use, the fencing, continuous occupation without interruption, absence of any permission or licence from the council, absence of any written acknowledgment of the council's title (LA 1980 s 29)
Obtain a corresponding declaration from the predecessor vendor who first occupied the strip (or their personal representatives if deceased)
Seek supporting declarations from neighbouring owners who can corroborate the exclusivity and continuity of occupation
Step 3 — Apply to HMLR

If the council's title is registered:

Apply under LRA 2002 sch 12 para 18 to alter the register. Form AP1 (Application to change register) is used, supported by the statutory declarations, a plan clearly identifying the strip, and a covering letter citing sch 12 para 18 and LRA 1925 s 75(1). HMLR will serve notice on the council as registered proprietor. If the council objects, the matter is referred to the First-tier Tribunal (Property Chamber) — Land Registration Division. Obtain a priority search (form OS1 or OS2) immediately to protect priority throughout the application period.

If the council's title is unregistered:

Apply by first registration on the basis of adverse possession using form ADV1 (Application to register adverse possession of unregistered land), supported by the statutory declarations and any other evidence of continuous occupation since 1986. HMLR will register a possessory freehold title (LRA 2002 s 11). As the council's title was extinguished under LA 1980 s 17 around 1998, it has no paper title capable of grounding a successful objection.

ADV1 timeline: HMLR targets approximately 30 working days for routine first registration applications, but adverse possession applications involving potential objections commonly take 4–12 months or longer. Where a council objects and the matter is referred to the First-tier Tribunal, a hearing may not be listed for 12–18 months. Exchange should therefore not be timed around a quick resolution.

Step 4 — Consider title indemnity insurance if exchange cannot wait

Where the HMLR application cannot be completed before exchange, specialist title indemnity insurance is the standard bridging mechanism. The vendor's position is particularly insurable because:

occupation extends approximately 40 years — well in excess of the 12-year minimum
the limitation period ran to completion in approximately 1998, five years before the LRA 2002 transitional provisions took effect
there is no evidence of any acknowledgment of the council's title or payment of rent
Insurers will require sight of the statutory declarations, the SIM result, and a summary of the council's correspondence. The policy should cover the buyer and, if there is a mortgage, the mortgagee.

Step 5 — Respond to the council and manage the dispute

Write formally requiring citation of statutory authority for any inspection (see above)
Give notice of the adverse possession entitlement and intended HMLR application
Assess whether without-prejudice negotiations for a deed of release or boundary agreement are commercially sensible — these are almost always faster and cheaper than a Tribunal reference and will maximise the vendor's prospects of a timely exchange
Step 6 — Inform and protect the buyer

The title defect must be disclosed to the buyer's solicitor in full and promptly — Standard Commercial Property Conditions, 3rd ed, condition 3.1.2 requires the seller to deduce a good root of title
Provide the buyer's solicitor with copies of the statutory declarations, the SIM result, and evidence of either the HMLR application or title indemnity insurance before exchange
Exchange should not proceed without the buyer's informed agreement on the mechanism (HMLR application or insurance) and any relevant special condition

Overall assessment: The vendor's position is strong. The 12-year period completed around 1998, five years before the LRA 2002 transitional cut-off. LRA 2002 sch 12 para 18 preserves the entitlement to registration, gives a statutory defence to any possession action, and requires the court to order registration if the matter is litigated. The council's inspection demand should be resisted pending citation of specific statutory authority. The primary task before exchange is to pursue the HMLR application under sch 12 para 18 (registered council title) or an ADV1 first registration (unregistered), and to obtain title indemnity insurance as a contingency if exchange timing requires it.
